Note · October 2026

Timestral with a ruler and a pencil

We tested Mistral AI's new Timestral models in all four sizes, along with five other families of foundation models, on series simple enough to forecast with a ruler and a pencil, where we know the best forecast anyone could make.

Dorian Joubaud

In short

  • Of the six families of foundation models we tested, Timestral gets closest to the best possible forecast on our 76 main tasks. The error of Timestral 209M is on average 1.54 times the best possible, against 1.72 for TimesFM 3.0, with the largest Toto 2.0 at 1.80 and Chronos-2 at 1.86. The gap to Chronos-2 comes from the series with 1 % noise, as with 10 % noise the two are level.
  • Its 80 % band collapses beyond 1 024 steps, because the package feeds the median of each block back into the context as if it had been observed. Toto 2.0 and Toto 1.0 show that this feedback is the cause, since the same weights keep most of their band over these horizons and lose it as soon as a single value is fed back. Feeding back nine quantiles instead of the median, as Chronos-2 does, gives Timestral much of its band back without any retraining. TimesFM 3.0 forecasts the whole horizon in one pass, and over the last block of a random walk its band still contains the truth 76 % of the time.
  • Timestral makes almost full use of a series that runs 8 steps ahead of a random walk, but like Chronos-2 and both Toto models, it all but ignores one that runs 96 steps ahead, although that series holds the whole future. TimesFM 3.0 uses part of it, cutting its error by 46 %. Shifted by its lead and passed as a covariate known in advance, the same series cuts the error of Timestral by 96 %.
  • Forecasting the logarithm helps a lot on fast growth, and the clip at zero that Timestral applies by default is best turned off for series that can go negative.

The best possible forecast

Each of our tasks draws 64 to 256 series from a process we know, so we also know the best forecast possible, made with that process and its parameters. Its 80 % band, between its 10 % and 90 % quantiles, appears as a dashed pair of lines on every plot. We score a model by dividing its quantile loss by that of this best forecast. A score of 1 is therefore perfect, and a score of 2 means twice the error that no model could avoid.

How close each model gets

Besides Timestral in its four sizes, we ran five other families of foundation models. Chronos-2 from Amazon, TimesFM 2.5 and 3.0 from Google, and Toto 2.0 from Datadog in its five sizes output quantiles directly, like Timestral. Toto 1.0, the previous Toto, is generative instead. For each patch of 64 steps it predicts a distribution and draws from it before moving on, so we draw 256 paths and read its quantiles off them, which is also why its median is dashed on the plots.

Timestral 209M does better than Chronos-2 on 53 of the 76 tasks, than TimesFM 2.5 on 58 and than TimesFM 3.0 on 43. Its 28M version is already on par with Chronos-2, a model about four times its size. Among the other families, TimesFM 3.0 comes closest, at 1.72, and beats Chronos-2 on 40 tasks. The largest Toto 2.0, with 2.5 billion parameters, follows at 1.80, beating Chronos-2 on 49 tasks but Timestral 209M on only 26, and each Toto 2.0 size does better than the one below it. Toto 1.0 is further behind, at 2.23, although it still beats Chronos-2 on 18 tasks. AutoETS and AutoARIMA, which we ran without seasonality, end far behind every foundation model, at 8.7 and 6.7 times the best possible.

The gap between Timestral and Chronos-2 comes from the 32 tasks with 1 % noise, where Timestral 209M is at 2.24 times the best possible against 3.49 for Chronos-2. With 10 % noise the two are level, at 1.21 and 1.22, and on the twelve random processes every foundation model lands between 1.06 and 1.08. Timestral was pre-trained on synthetic series alone, before a post-training on real data, so our series may sit closer to its training data than to that of the other models.

Straight lines

With 1 % noise, Timestral handles straight lines very well, with an error between 1.2 and 1.5 times the best possible at every size, against 1.7 to 1.9 for Chronos-2, except on a line falling through zero, which its default clip holds at zero. Toto 1.0 does as well, at 1.1 to 1.4, and with 10 % noise Timestral and Chronos-2 both stay within 8 % of the best possible on the other lines. Timestral standardises each patch with the mean and spread of every value up to the end of that patch. On a rising line, every patch then ends close to √3 standard deviations above this running mean, whatever the slope, so all rising lines look alike to the network. This may help, although we have not tested it. Toto 1.0, Toto 2.0 and TimesFM 2.5 standardise their input the same way, and only the two Toto models match Timestral on rising lines. TimesFM 3.0 does better than all of them, within 1 % of the best possible on every line except the one falling through zero, where its own clamp at zero costs it as much as Timestral's. Before forecasting, TimesFM 3.0 removes a straight line fitted on the history whenever that line explains most of it, and extends the line over the horizon.

With only 128 steps of history, Chronos-2's median also follows the line, but its band is twelve times wider than Timestral's and sags far below the line, and its error is 9.7 times the best possible against 2.2 for Timestral and 1.04 for TimesFM 3.0.

Exponential growth

On exponentials, the error of every model grows with the growth rate. With growth of e³ over the context, the median of Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5 ends below the truth on every series, that of TimesFM 3.0 on 99 % of them, and Timestral's on only 9 %, as it ends above the truth instead. On faster growth its error, 5.3 and 15 times the best possible at e⁵ and e¹⁰, is above that of Chronos-2 and about twice that of the largest Toto 2.0. Forecasting the logarithm and then exponentiating the quantiles turns the curve into a line, and Timestral's error then stays between 1.1 and 2.1 times the best possible. The largest Toto 2.0 also gains from it when the growth is fast, going from 6.9 to 1.6 times the best possible at e¹⁰. With 8 192 steps of history, where the growth over the context reaches e⁴⁸, or 4 096 steps ahead, the logarithm makes Timestral's error worse instead.

Breaks, gaps and slow cycles

Timestral follows a recent level shift and crosses a gap in its history at little cost, whereas after a shift Chronos-2's band still reaches back to the old level. TimesFM 2.5 does better than Timestral on a slow cycle of period 300, at 2.1 times the best possible against 3.8 with 512 steps of history, and Timestral only catches up with 4 096 steps. With 512 steps, TimesFM 3.0 sits in between on that cycle, at 2.7, but follows a level shift far less well than Timestral, at 9.2 times the best possible against 1.6. No model copes with a chirp, whose period keeps shrinking.

Long horizons

Timestral forecasts up to 1 024 steps in one pass, and for longer horizons the package appends the median of that first block to the context and forecasts the next block from there. The model reads that median as if it had been observed, so the next block starts from a known point and the uncertainty built up over the first block is lost. A median is also smooth, so the model sees a calm recent past and widens its band again only slowly. On a random walk, the 80 % band contains the truth 79 % of the time over the first block, then 17, 7 and 2 % over the next three. An exact forecaster of the random walk, fed its own median in the same way, would still reach 50, 40 and 35 % over those blocks. Chronos-2 also decodes by blocks of 1 024 steps but feeds back nine quantiles rather than the median, and its band still contains the truth 67 % of the time over the last block. The two Toto models, which decode in one pass or with sampled paths, stay close to 60 %. TimesFM 3.0 forecasts the whole horizon in one pass. Its band contains the truth 76 % of the time over the last block, as often as the best possible band on these 64 walks, but it is about 1.5 times as wide.

TimesFM 2.5 also feeds its median back, every 128 steps, but its recommended quantile head takes the width of its band from the real history alone. On a random walk that band still contains the truth 87 % of the time from step 129 to step 1 024, where the head stops. Beyond, only its base head is left, which reads its band off the context with the median fed back, and the collapse comes earlier. Its band then contains the truth of a random walk 79 % of the time over the first 128 steps, 3 % over the rest of the first block and almost never after. On a line, the medians go wrong in opposite directions, as Timestral curves up after the first block while Chronos-2 bends down within it, so that Timestral ends above the truth and Chronos-2 below it on every series.

TimesFM 3.0 stays on the line, at the best possible error 4 096 steps ahead, because it removes the straight line fitted on the history and extends it. On random walks the same step misleads it, as it removes a line on 12 of the 64 walks, where its error is 2.4 times the best possible against 1.06 on the others. Over all the walks its error is then 1.32 times the best possible, against 1.26 for Timestral and 1.05 for Chronos-2. On a sine of period 300, its forecast leaves the curve after step 3 072, and its band then contains the truth a third of the time.

Why the band collapses

Toto 2.0 lets us check that the feedback is the cause, because the same weights can forecast a long horizon either in one pass or by blocks of 768 steps, with the median fed back between blocks as its README recommends beyond about 1 000 steps. Only the decoding changes, and both give the same forecast over the first 768 steps. With the median fed back, the band of Toto 2.0 2.5B shrinks between steps 768 and 769 from 0.99 to 0.004 times the width of the best possible band. It widens again within each block without ever reaching the right width, so that beyond step 768 it contains the truth of a random walk 14 % of the time, against 63 % in one pass, and the four smaller sizes also lose their band at step 769 when their median is fed back.

Feeding back the median does keep the median of the largest Toto 2.0 closer to the truth far out, the long-term stability its README describes. On a sine of period 24, 4 096 steps ahead, its error is 1.4 times the best possible with the median fed back, against about 7 in one pass.

Toto 1.0 is generative and predicts no quantiles. Its usual decoding feeds each of its 256 paths with its own draws, so its band holds and still contains the truth 62 % of the time after step 64. When the same weights are fed the mean of each patch instead, the band collapses one patch later and contains the truth less than 1 % of the time after step 64, so the collapse does not come from predicting quantiles.

A fix without retraining

In every model we tried, a band read off a context that ends with a single fed-back value collapses. Chronos-2 avoids it by feeding back nine quantile paths, one per decile, instead of one value. After the first block it makes nine copies of the context, appends a different decile of the block to each copy, forecasts the next block from every copy and pools the nine forecasts into one set of quantiles. Its code describes this as a better heuristic "than just using the median to unroll, which leads to uncertainty collapse" (chronos-forecasting, chronos2/pipeline.py), and notes that it still shrinks the probability space, which shows here as a band that narrows from block to block.

Since all of this happens in the decoding loop, outside the network, any model that outputs quantiles can use it without touching its weights. We ported it and ran Timestral 209M through it, keeping its blocks of 1 024 steps. Nothing changes within the first block, and after it the band holds far better. Over the last three blocks of the random walk, it contains the truth 73, 65 and 57 % of the time, against 17, 7 and 2 % as shipped.

On the random walk and the AR(1), the gain comes from the band alone, as the median barely moves. Over 4 096 steps, Timestral's error falls from 1.26 to 1.05 times the best possible on the random walk and from 1.22 to 1.01 on the AR(1), which puts it level with Chronos-2. Its band is still too narrow on the random walk, since over the last block it contains the truth 57 % of the time, where the best possible band reaches 76 % on these 64 series.

On the line, the paths make the upward drift of the median worse, and the error rises by 14 % at 4 096 steps and by 45 % at 2 048. At 4 096 steps the band contains the truth again only because it grows about four times wider over the last block. On the two sines, of periods 24 and 300, the error rises by 2 to 20 %. The exponential gains, by 17 and 54 %, but both forecasts stay 16 to 86 times the best possible there, so over the long tasks the paths pay off on the random walk and the AR(1) and cost on the line and the sines.

The method is also slower, as every block after the first needs nine forward passes instead of one, which takes 37 seconds instead of 3 on the random walk. On the largest Toto 2.0 it only slows the collapse of the band on the random walk, to 51, 36 and 20 % over the last three blocks of 1 024 steps, where one pass keeps 70, 59 and 58 %, although the paths lower its error on 9 of the 12 long tasks.

A series running ahead of the target

Researchers at Layer 6 found that Chronos-2, Moirai and Toto make little use of a covariate that runs ahead of the target, even when it holds the whole future (Ghoroghchian et al., 2026). We gave our models the same kind of series, the target itself 96 steps ahead, observed over the past only, so that its last 96 values are exactly the future to forecast. Neither Timestral nor Chronos-2 makes much use of it, but once shifted back by its lead, the same series becomes a covariate known in advance and both models use it well on the random walk and the AR(1). The lead is what matters, since with a series 8 steps ahead Timestral takes 90 % of the gain the best possible forecast gets on the random walk and 95 % on the AR(1), about a quarter with 32 steps, and none with 96. TimesFM 3.0 makes much more use of the series 96 steps ahead as given, with an error 46 % lower on the random walk and 36 % lower on the AR(1), although the best possible forecast makes no error at all on either.

Toto 2.0 takes no covariates, and Toto 1.0 only covariates known in advance, so we gave both the leading series as a second series, as Layer 6 did in SimpleTimeBench, their benchmark. Toto makes little use of it either, since with the series 96 steps ahead the error falls by only 3 % for Toto 2.0 2.5B and by 6 to 8 % for Toto 1.0, while the best possible error falls to zero.

When the target is simply 2c + 1 for a covariate c known in advance (the first two tasks of the figure above), Timestral, Chronos-2 and TimesFM 3.0 all use c, with errors 5 to 11 % of those without it. A covariate unrelated to the target hardly bothers Timestral, TimesFM 3.0 or the largest Toto 2.0, whose error changes by at most 7 %, but it raises the error by 12 to 29 % for Chronos-2.

Findings

About the models

  • On our 76 main tasks, Timestral 209M comes closest to the best possible forecast, with an error 1.54 times as large on average, against 1.72 for TimesFM 3.0, with the largest Toto 2.0 at 1.80 and Chronos-2 at 1.86. Its 28M version already matches Chronos-2, and the gap to Chronos-2 comes from the series with 1 % noise.
  • With 1 % noise, it handles straight lines very well, at 1.2 to 1.5 times the best possible at every size unless a line falls through zero with the clip on, close to Toto 1.0 and ahead of Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5. TimesFM 3.0 does better, within 1 % of the best possible except on the line falling through zero, as it removes a fitted line before forecasting. Timestral's band also stays much tighter than that of Chronos-2 when the history is short.
  • As shipped, it does worse than Chronos-2 on fast exponential growth, with about twice the error of the largest Toto 2.0, until the logarithm is forecast instead.
  • It follows level shifts and crosses gaps well, while TimesFM 2.5 does better on a slow cycle of period 300 with 512 to 2 048 steps of history, and no model copes with a chirp.
  • Beyond 1 024 steps its band collapses, because the package feeds the median of each block back into the context as if it had been observed.
  • In every model we tried, a band read off a context that ends with a single fed-back value collapses. With the same weights, Toto 2.0 keeps most of its band in one pass and loses it at step 769 when its median is fed back, and Toto 1.0 keeps it with sampled paths but loses it when fed its mean. TimesFM 2.5 also feeds its median back, but its recommended head takes the width of its band from the real history and keeps it. Fed back, the median of Toto 2.0 does stay closer to the truth far out than in one pass.
  • TimesFM 3.0 forecasts the whole horizon in one pass, and over the last block of a random walk its band contains the truth 76 % of the time, as often as the best possible band, but it is about 1.5 times as wide. Its median drifts on some of the walks, because it extends a straight line fitted on the history whenever that line explains most of it.
  • Feeding back quantile paths as Chronos-2 does gives Timestral much of its band back without retraining. Over the last block of a random walk, its band then contains the truth 57 % of the time instead of 2 %, and its error falls from 1.26 to 1.05. The paths lower the error by 10 to 17 % on the random walk and the AR(1), but they raise it by 2 to 45 % on the line and the sines and need nine forward passes for each block after the first.
  • Timestral uses a series 8 steps ahead of a random walk almost fully, but Timestral, Chronos-2 and both Toto models make little use of one that runs 96 steps ahead, given as a covariate or as a second series, although it holds the whole future. TimesFM 3.0 uses part of it, cutting its error by 46 % on the random walk and 36 % on the AR(1).

If you use Timestral

  • For fast growth, forecast the logarithm and exponentiate the quantiles, which keeps the error on the exponentials of the main grid between 1.0 and 2.1 times the best possible. With 8 192 steps of history or 4 096 steps ahead, it made the error worse.
  • Beyond 1 024 steps, the band as shipped is far too narrow, so either stay within one block or feed back quantile paths as Chronos-2 does.
  • Shift a leading series by its lead and pass it as a known covariate when the lead covers the whole horizon, which cut the error by 76 to 97 % in our tasks. With a shorter lead, Timestral copies the known steps and then jumps away, and it ended worse than without the covariate in five of our six cases, so forecast only as far as the lead.
  • For series that can go below zero, set clip_nonnegative=False. By default, Timestral clamps at zero the forecast of any series whose past is non-negative, which suits demand or energy but flattens a line falling through zero, at 14.6 times the best possible error instead of 1.3. The README does not mention this default, only the docstring of forecast does.
  • Keep symmetric_averaging on. It averages the forecast of the series with the flipped forecast of its negative, and without it the error of Timestral 209M on sines with 1 % noise rises by 9 to 26 %.
Method details
  • About 190 000 target series in 330 tasks, and every model that runs a task sees the same series. The main grid has 512 steps of context and 96 of horizon, with each of 32 shapes at 1 % and at 10 % noise, relative to the spread of the signal, and 12 random processes. Further grids vary the context from 128 to 8 192 steps and the horizon up to 4 096, and add covariates.
  • We ran Timestral as shipped, then with each of its two test-time options turned off (symmetric_averaging and clip_nonnegative), on the logarithm of positive series, and through quantile paths. Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5 ran with their recommended settings and the Toto models with their defaults, next to a least-squares line (our ruler) and to AutoETS and AutoARIMA, non-seasonal, on the last 1 024 steps.
  • TimesFM 3.0 ran with the defaults of the class its benchmarks use (TimesFM3Evaluator), which turn on the same two options as Timestral: each forecast is averaged with the flipped forecast of the negated series, and the forecast of a series whose past is non-negative is clamped at zero. With the clamp off, its error on the main grid falls from 1.72 to 1.65 times the best possible.
  • A leader shifted by fewer steps than the horizon leaves the rest of the horizon unknown, and we pass those steps as missing values. Timestral and Chronos-2 treat them as missing, but the package of TimesFM 3.0 fills them with the last known value, so the model reads a flat line as the known future. We therefore show TimesFM 3.0 with a shifted leader only when the lead covers the whole horizon.
  • Toto 2.0 comes in five sizes, from 4M to 2.5B parameters, and Toto 1.0 has 151M. Both read at most the last 4 096 steps of context. The draws of Toto 1.0 are seeded, and without a seed its error moves by about 2 % at most. With its mean fed back, the quantiles of each patch are read off 256 draws of the distribution it predicts.
  • The score is the quantile loss over the nine deciles, scaled per series by the error of the one-step naive forecast and divided by that of the best possible forecast. The quantiles of Chronos-2 and Toto 2.0 can cross, so we sort them, which never increases the loss. The plots show the 10 %, 50 % and 90 % quantiles.
  • On SimpleTimeBench, which we regenerated, our Chronos-2 lands where Layer 6 reported it on their measure of bias towards the mean, a check of our pipeline. On its lines, Timestral's forecast leans towards the mean of the context on 44 % of the steps, against 99 % for Chronos-2, where an unbiased forecast would on half of them.
  • Through our port of the quantile paths, Chronos-2 matches its own rollout, with the same coverage per block to three decimals and errors within 1 %.
  • We used timestral 1.0.0, chronos-forecasting 2.3.2, timesfm 3.0.2 (for TimesFM 2.5 and 3.0), toto-2 2.0.0, toto-ts 0.1.4 and statsforecast 2.1.1.
