Note · October 2026

Timestral with a ruler and a pencil

Mistral AI's new forecasting models, all four sizes, on series a person could forecast by hand, scored against the best forecast possible.

Dorian Joubaud

In short

  • Of the five families of foundation models we tested, Timestral gets closest to the best possible forecast. On our 76 main tasks, the error of Timestral 209M is on average 1.54 times the smallest possible, against 1.86 for Chronos-2.
  • Its 80 % band collapses beyond 1 024 steps, because the package feeds the median of each block back into the context. Toto 2.0 and Toto 1.0 keep their band over the same horizons and lose it as soon as a single value is fed back. Feeding back quantile paths instead, as Chronos-2 does, gives Timestral most of its band back without any retraining.
  • Like Chronos-2 and both Toto models, Timestral ignores a series that runs ahead of the target, even when that series holds the whole future. Shifted by its lead and passed as a covariate known in advance, the same series cuts its error by about 96 %.
  • Forecasting the logarithm helps a lot on fast growth, and the clip at zero that Timestral applies by default is best turned off for series that can go negative.

The best possible forecast

Each task draws hundreds of series from a process we know, so we also know the best forecast anyone could make, and its 80 % band appears as a dashed pair of lines on every plot. We score a model by dividing its quantile loss by that of this best forecast. A score of 1 is therefore perfect, and a score of 2 means twice the error that no model could avoid.

How close each model gets

Besides Timestral in its four sizes, we ran four other families of foundation models. Chronos-2 from Amazon, TimesFM 2.5 from Google and Toto 2.0 from Datadog in its five sizes output quantiles directly, like Timestral. Toto 1.0, the previous Toto, is generative instead. For each patch of 64 steps it predicts a distribution and draws from it before moving on, so we draw 256 paths and read its quantiles off them, which is also why its median is dashed on the plots.

Timestral 209M does better than Chronos-2 on 53 of the 76 tasks and better than TimesFM 2.5 on 58, and its 28M version, about a quarter of the size of Chronos-2, is already on par with it. The largest Toto 2.0, with 2.5 billion parameters, comes next, beating Chronos-2 on 49 tasks but Timestral 209M on only 26, and each Toto 2.0 size does better than the one below it. Toto 1.0 is further behind, at 2.23, although it still beats Chronos-2 on 18 tasks.

Straight lines

Timestral handles straight lines very well, with an error between 1.2 and 1.5 times the best possible at every size, against 1.7 to 1.9 for Chronos-2. This may come from the way it normalises its input, although we have not tested it. Timestral standardises each patch with the mean and spread of the series up to the end of that patch, and in that frame every rising line looks the same to the network whatever its slope, so extending the line amounts to holding a level. With only 128 steps of history, Chronos-2's median also follows the line, but its band is twelve times wider than Timestral's and sags toward the past.

Exponential growth

On exponentials, the error of every model grows with the growth rate. With growth of e³ over the context, the median of Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5 ends below the truth on every series, and Timestral's on only 9 % of them. Forecasting the logarithm and then exponentiating the quantiles turns the curve into a line, and Timestral's error then stays between 1.1 and 2.1 times the best possible. Toto 2.0 also gains from it when the growth is fast, going from 6.9 to 1.6 times the best possible at e¹⁰.

Breaks, gaps and slow cycles

Timestral follows a recent level shift and crosses a gap in its history at little cost, whereas after a shift Chronos-2's band still reaches back to the old level. TimesFM 2.5 does better than Timestral on a slow cycle when the context holds less than two of its periods, and no model copes with a chirp, whose period keeps shrinking.

Long horizons

Timestral forecasts up to 1 024 steps in one pass, and for longer horizons the package appends the median of that first block to the context and forecasts the next block from there. A median is smooth, so the model then sees a calm recent past and narrows its band. On a random walk, the 80 % band contains the truth 79 % of the time over the first block, 17 % over the second and only 2 % over the last. Chronos-2 also decodes by blocks of 1 024 steps, yet its band still contains the truth 67 % of the time over the last block, and the two Toto models stay close to 60 %.

TimesFM 2.5 shows the same collapse earlier, because its recommended quantile head stops at 1 024 steps and beyond that its base head feeds the median back every 128 steps. Its band contains the truth of a random walk 79 % of the time over the first 128 steps, 3 % over the rest of the first block and almost never after. On a line, the medians go wrong in opposite directions, as Timestral curves up after the first block while Chronos-2 bends down within it, so that they end above and below the truth on every series.

Why the band collapses

Toto 2.0 lets us check that the feedback is the cause, because the same weights can forecast a long horizon either in one pass or by blocks of 768 steps, with the median fed back between blocks as its README recommends beyond about 1 000 steps. Only the decoding changes, and both give the same forecast over the first 768 steps. With the median fed back, the band of Toto 2.0 2.5B shrinks between steps 768 and 769 from 0.99 to 0.004 times the width of the best possible band. It widens again within each block without ever reaching the right width, so that beyond step 768 it contains the truth of a random walk 14 % of the time, against 63 % in one pass, and the four smaller sizes behave the same way.

Feeding back the median does have one advantage, as it keeps the median itself on track far out. On a sine of period 24, 4 096 steps ahead, the error of Toto 2.0 is 1.4 times the best possible with the median fed back, against 7.0 in one pass.

Toto 1.0, which is generative, shows that the problem does not lie in predicting quantiles. Its usual decoding feeds each of its 256 paths with its own draws, and its band holds, still containing the truth 64 % of the time over the last 1 024 steps. When the same weights are fed the mean of each patch instead, the band collapses one patch later and contains the truth only 1 % of the time between steps 65 and 1 024.

A fix without retraining

In every model we tried, feeding back a single central value collapses the band. Chronos-2 avoids it by feeding back nine values, one per decile. After the first block it makes nine copies of the context, appends a different decile of the block to each copy, forecasts the next block from every copy and pools the nine forecasts into one set of quantiles. Its code describes this as a better heuristic "than just using the median to unroll, which leads to uncertainty collapse" (chronos-forecasting, _autoregressive_unroll_for_long_horizon).

Since all of this happens in the decoder, any model can use it without touching its weights. We ported it and ran Timestral 209M through it, keeping its blocks of 1 024 steps. Nothing changes within the first block, and after it the band holds far better. Over the last three blocks of the random walk, it contains the truth 73, 65 and 57 % of the time, against 17, 7 and 2 % as shipped.

Over 4 096 steps, the quantile paths also improve Timestral's median on the random series. Its error falls from 1.26 to 1.05 times the best possible on the random walk and from 1.22 to 1.01 on the AR(1), level with Chronos-2. On a line and on sines, however, they cost 5 to 15 %, and the band remains too narrow, since it contains the truth 57 % of the time over the last block where the best possible band reaches 76 %.

The method is also slower, as every block after the first needs nine forward passes instead of one, which takes 37 seconds instead of 3 on the random walk. On Toto 2.0 it only slows the collapse down, to 51, 36 and 20 % over the last three blocks, and that model is better decoded in one pass.

A series running ahead of the target

Layer 6 found that Chronos-2, Moirai and Toto ignore a covariate that runs ahead of the target, even when it holds the whole future (Ghoroghchian et al., 2026). We gave our models the same kind of series, the target itself 96 steps ahead, observed over the past only, so that its last 96 values are exactly the future to forecast. Neither Timestral nor Chronos-2 makes use of it, but once shifted back by its lead, the same series becomes a covariate known in advance and both models use it well.

Toto takes no covariates, so we gave it the leading series as a second series, as Layer 6 did in SimpleTimeBench, their benchmark. Toto ignores it as well, since with the series 96 steps ahead the error of Toto 2.0 2.5B falls by only 3 % and that of Toto 1.0 by 8 %, while the best possible error falls to zero.

A covariate unrelated to the target hardly bothers Timestral or the largest Toto 2.0, whose error changes by 1 to 7 %, but it raises the error of Chronos-2 by 12 to 29 %.

Findings

About the models

  • Timestral 209M comes closest to the best possible forecast, with an error 1.54 times as large on average, against 1.80 for the largest Toto 2.0 and 1.86 for Chronos-2. Its 28M version already matches Chronos-2.
  • It handles straight lines very well, at 1.2 to 1.5 times the best possible at every size, and its band stays much tighter than that of Chronos-2 when the history is short.
  • It follows level shifts and crosses gaps well, while TimesFM 2.5 does better on a slow cycle seen for less than two periods, and no model copes with a chirp.
  • Beyond 1 024 steps its band collapses, because the package feeds the median of each block back into the context.
  • Feeding back a single value collapses the band in every model we tried. With the same weights, Toto 2.0 keeps most of its band in one pass and loses it at step 769 when its median is fed back, and Toto 1.0 keeps it with sampled paths but loses it when fed its mean. The median, on the other hand, holds better far out when it is fed back.
  • Feeding back quantile paths as Chronos-2 does gives Timestral most of its band back without retraining. Over the last block of a random walk, its band then contains the truth 57 % of the time instead of 2 %, and its error falls from 1.26 to 1.05, for a cost of 5 to 15 % on lines and sines and nine forward passes per block.
  • Timestral, Chronos-2 and both Toto models ignore a series that runs 96 steps ahead of the target, given as a covariate or as a second series, although it holds the whole future.

If you use Timestral

  • For fast growth, forecast the logarithm and exponentiate the quantiles, which keeps the error on exponentials between 1.1 and 2.1 times the best possible.
  • Beyond 1 024 steps, do not trust the band as shipped, and either stay within one block or feed back quantile paths as Chronos-2 does.
  • Shift a leading series by its lead and pass it as a known covariate. Given as it is, the series is ignored, while shifted it cuts the error by about 96 % when its lead covers the horizon.
  • For series that can go below zero, set clip_nonnegative=False. By default, Timestral clamps at zero the forecast of any series whose past is non-negative, which suits demand or energy but holds a falling line at zero, at 14.6 times the best possible error instead of 1.3.
  • Keep symmetric_averaging on. It averages the forecast of the series with the flipped forecast of its negative, and without it the error of the 209M on sines with 1 % noise rises by 9 to 26 %.
Method details
  • We ran Timestral as shipped, then with each of its two test-time options turned off (symmetric_averaging and clip_nonnegative), on the logarithm of positive series, and through quantile paths. Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5 ran with their recommended settings and Toto 2.0 and Toto 1.0 with their defaults, next to a least-squares line (our ruler), AutoETS and AutoARIMA.
  • Timestral 1.0.0 (inference code at a534bdb), with its weights at their Hugging Face revisions of 15 October 2026. Chronos-2 through chronos-forecasting 2.3.2 and TimesFM 2.5 through timesfm 3.0.2. Chronos-2's quantiles can cross, so we sort them, which never increases the loss. AutoETS and AutoARIMA come from statsforecast 2.1.1, non-seasonal, on the last 1 024 steps.
  • Toto 2.0 in its five sizes (4M to 2.5B) through toto-2 2.0.0, and Toto 1.0 (151M) through toto-ts 0.1.4, with their weights at their Hugging Face revisions of 22 October, on every task they can take. They take no covariates, so a leading series is given to them as a second series. Their context is cut to its last 4 096 steps and to a multiple of their patch (32 and 64 steps). Toto 2.0 decodes in one pass by default, and by blocks of 768 steps with the median fed back for the comparison above. Its quantiles are sorted, like those of Chronos-2.
  • Toto 1.0 draws 256 paths and its quantiles are read off them, so they carry sampling noise. Its draws are seeded, and without a seed its error moved by 0.05 % on the median task and by 2 % at most. For the comparison above, the same weights also ran with the mean of each patch fed back, the loop of its generate_mean, the quantiles of each patch then being read off 256 draws of the distribution it predicts.
  • The quantile paths are our port of _autoregressive_unroll_for_long_horizon from chronos-forecasting 2.3.2, with the nine deciles as paths and the same pooling weights. Timestral runs through it with blocks of 1 024 steps and Toto 2.0 with blocks of 768. Run through our port, Chronos-2 matches its own rollout, with the same coverage per block to three decimals and errors within 1 %.
  • About 190 000 target series in 330 settings, drawn once from seeds derived from the setting names so that every model sees the same series. The main grid has 512 steps of context, 96 of horizon and noise at 1 % of the spread of the signal. Further grids vary the context from 128 to 8 192 steps and the horizon up to 4 096, and add covariates.
  • We also regenerated SimpleTimeBench, the benchmark of Layer 6, with its own code at a7d6c6e. Our Chronos-2 lands where Layer 6 reported it, which checks our pipeline. On its lines, Timestral's forecast leans toward the mean of the context on 44 % of the steps, against 99 % for Chronos-2.
  • The score is the quantile loss over the nine deciles, scaled by the error of the one-step naive forecast on the context and divided by that of the best possible forecast. We compare settings with this ratio only. The plots show the 10 %, 50 % and 90 % quantiles.
  • Everything ran on the MeluXina supercomputer, on nodes with four A100 GPUs. Timestral, Chronos-2, TimesFM 2.5 and the classical methods took 25 minutes on one node, Toto 2.0 38 minutes, and Toto 1.0 about 6 GPU-hours for its 256 paths. Beyond 1 024 steps, TimesFM 2.5 runs with its base quantile head, because timesfm 3.0.2 refuses longer horizons with the continuous one.
