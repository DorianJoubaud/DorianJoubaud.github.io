Note · October 2026

Timestral with a ruler and a pencil

Mistral AI's new forecasting models, all four sizes, on series a person could forecast by hand, scored against the best forecast possible.

Dorian Joubaud · code, data and pre-registered predictions: timestral-probes

In short

  • On 76 simple tasks, Timestral 209M is the closest of three foundation models to the best possible forecast: its error is 1.54 times the unavoidable one on average, against 1.86 for Chronos-2 and 2.02 for TimesFM 2.5. Timestral 28M is on par with Chronos-2.
  • Lines, cycles, gaps and level shifts are handled well. Unlike the models Layer 6 tested, Timestral does not drift back to the mean on trends.
  • A line that should cross zero is held at zero. A default option clamps the forecast of any series whose past is non-negative, and turning it off removes the error.
  • Beyond 1 024 steps the uncertainty band collapses: the 80 % band covers 79 % of a random walk over the first block, then 17 % over the next.
  • Exponentials are not damped as in the other models, but the error still grows with the growth rate, and forecasting the logarithm removes most of it.
  • A covariate known in advance is used. A series that leads the target is ignored, unless it is shifted back by its lead and passed as a known covariate, which cuts the error to a few percent when the lead covers the horizon.

Why simple series

Time series foundation models now top the public forecasting benchmarks. Yet a recent study from Layer 6 found that three of them return to the mean on trends, smooth out exponential growth and ignore a covariate that tells them the future (Ghoroghchian et al., 2026). Timestral, which Mistral AI released on 15 October, was pre-trained on synthetic data only, so the question is fair: has it learned what a ruler and a pencil would do?

We tested its four sizes (5M, 28M, 123M and 209M parameters) on series where a person would forecast well, and where the best possible forecast is known exactly.

How we tested

Each task draws hundreds of series from a known process: a line, an exponential, a cycle, a random walk, a cycle with a gap. Because the process is known, so is the best forecast anyone could make, which we call the oracle. For a line with noise, it is the line itself with the noise band around it. For a random walk, it is the last value, with a band that widens like the square root of the horizon.

Each model is scored by its quantile loss over the deciles, divided by the oracle's. A ratio of 1 is the best possible, a ratio of 2 means twice the unavoidable error. Unless stated, the context has 512 steps and the horizon 96, and the noise is 1 % of the signal's spread. Further grids vary the context from 128 to 8 192 steps and the horizon up to 4 096, and add covariates.

Timestral runs as shipped, then with each of its two test-time options turned off, and on the logarithm of positive series. Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5 run on the same series with their recommended settings, next to classical methods such as a least-squares line and AutoETS. We also regenerated SimpleTimeBench, the Layer 6 benchmark, with its own code. Our Chronos-2 lands where Layer 6 reported it, which checks the pipeline.

Before running anything, we read Timestral's inference code and wrote down six predictions, committed with the code. The last sections say which held.

See for yourself

Closest to the best possible

The main grid has 76 tasks with a positive unavoidable error: lines, curves, cycles, breaks, noise and random walks, at two noise levels. Averaged over them, Timestral 209M is the closest to the oracle.

Head to head, Timestral 209M has the lower error on 53 of the 76 tasks against Chronos-2, and on 58 against TimesFM 2.5. Timestral 28M, a quarter of Chronos-2's size, is on par with it (33 of 76), which matches what Mistral reports on GIFT-Eval. The classical models are far behind, mostly because a non-seasonal ETS or ARIMA cannot follow a cycle. On pure noise and on random walks without drift, every foundation model is within 4 % of the oracle.

Why lines may be easy for Timestral

Timestral standardises each patch of 32 steps with the mean and standard deviation of everything it has seen so far, then squashes it with asinh. In this frame, a straight line looks flat: its standardised value tends to √3, about 1.73, whatever its slope and intercept. Continuing a line then amounts to staying at the same level. We did not test this explanation directly, but the forecasts fit it.

On lines, Timestral's error is 1.2 to 1.5 times the oracle's at every size, against 1.7 to 1.9 for Chronos-2. The gap is widest with short contexts: with 128 steps of history, Timestral 209M is at 2.2 times the oracle and Chronos-2 at 9.7. On SimpleTimeBench's lines, Timestral's forecast leans toward the context mean on 44 % of the steps, close to the unbiased 50 %, where Chronos-2 does so on 99 %. A parabola, which looks flat in the same frame, is harder: only the 209M stays within twice the oracle.

Exponentials: not damped, but the error grows

Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5 damp exponential growth: from a growth of e³ over the context, their median ends below the truth on every series. Timestral does not damp. It overshoots moderate growth slightly and is nearly unbiased on average at e¹⁰. Its error still grows with the growth, from 1.2 times the oracle at e¹ to 15 times at e¹⁰, much like Chronos-2.

The fix is old: forecast the logarithm, then exponentiate the quantiles. The exponential becomes a line, which Timestral handles well, and the error falls to between 1.1 and 2.1 times the oracle.

Crossing zero: a default option

By default, Timestral's forecast clamps at zero the forecast of every series whose observed past is non-negative. It is a sensible guard for demand or energy, but it breaks a line that is about to go below zero: the error is 14.6 times the oracle's, against 1.3 on the mirror image of the same task. TimesFM 2.5, whose recommended setting does the same, behaves alike. With clip_nonnegative=False, Timestral is back at 1.3.

The other default averages the forecast with the reflected forecast of the negated series, and it helps. Turning it off raises the error on sines by 5 to 29 % depending on the size, and changes nothing on noise.

Long horizons: the band collapses after one block

Timestral forecasts up to 1 024 steps in one pass. Beyond, it appends that block's median to the context and forecasts the next block from it. The collapse starts exactly there, at step 1 025. Our reading is that a median is smooth, so the model then sees a calm recent past and returns a narrow band. On a random walk, the 80 % band covers 79 % of the truth over the first 1 024 steps, then 17, 7 and 2 % over the next three blocks. Chronos-2, which rolls long horizons out from nine quantile paths, keeps between 67 and 81 %.

Within one block, Timestral is the better of the two. On a line forecast 1 024 steps ahead, its MASE is 0.86, close to the oracle's 0.71, where Chronos-2's is 7.8.

Covariates: known in advance yes, leading no

When the target is twice a covariate plus one, and the covariate is known over the horizon, Timestral's error falls to 6 to 8 % of its error without it, Chronos-2's to 8 to 11 %. When the covariate is the target itself 96 steps ahead, given over the past only, its last 96 values are the whole future. Neither model uses it: Timestral's error moves by less than 2 %, Chronos-2's falls by 14 % at most. This is the blind spot Layer 6 found in Chronos-2, Moirai and Toto, and Timestral shares it.

There is a way around it when the leader runs at least as far ahead as the horizon. Shifted back by its lead, the leading series becomes a covariate known in advance over the next steps, which Timestral does use. With a leader 96 steps ahead and a 96-step horizon, its error then falls to 3 or 4 % of the error without it, against 8 to 13 % for Chronos-2. On SimpleTimeBench's random walk with its lagged copy, the error relative to a flat forecast drops from 1.02 to 0.10. With a shorter lead, the shifted covariate stops before the horizon ends, and Timestral then does worse than without it in five of our six cases, by up to a factor 3.8 on a sine. We tried this after the main study, so it was not among the predictions.

An unrelated covariate, an independent random walk, changes Timestral's error by 1 to 7 % and raises Chronos-2's by 12 to 29 %.

Breaks, gaps and slow cycles

Timestral adapts to a recent level shift, at 1.6 times the oracle, where Chronos-2 holds on to the old level and reaches 37 times. A block of missing values covering a tenth of the context costs it almost nothing. With fewer than two periods of a slow cycle in the context, TimesFM 2.5 does better (2.1 against 3.8 times the oracle), and from 1 024 steps of context both are close to the oracle. Every model fails on a chirp, whose period keeps shrinking.

What we predicted, and what held

We committed six predictions before the first run, from the code alone. One held outright, the others failed on at least one of their criteria. The one we got most wrong is the exponentials: we expected damping, and found overshooting.

What this means

For people using Timestral:

  • Pass clip_nonnegative=False when a series that is positive today can go below zero, such as a balance, a net flow or a difference.
  • For exponential growth, forecast the logarithm.
  • Within 1 024 steps the bands are reliable. Beyond, treat them as far too narrow.
  • A series that leads the target by at least the horizon is worth shifting back by its lead and passing as a known covariate. With a shorter lead, forecast only as far as the lead.

For the model itself, two of the weak points are in the decoding rather than in the network: the default clip, and the median fed back between blocks. Rolling out from quantile paths, as Chronos-2 does, would likely keep the band. In our tests Timestral does not exploit a series that leads the target, which may be a question for its synthetic pre-training.

Method details

  • Timestral 1.0.0 (inference code at a534bdb), weights at their Hugging Face revisions of 15 October 2026. Chronos-2 through chronos-forecasting 2.3.2, TimesFM 2.5 through timesfm 3.0.2, both with their recommended settings. Chronos-2's quantiles can cross, so they are sorted, which never increases the loss. AutoETS and AutoARIMA from statsforecast 2.1.1, non-seasonal, on the last 1 024 steps.
  • About 190 000 target series in 330 settings, drawn once from seeds derived from the setting names, so that every model sees the same series. SimpleTimeBench was regenerated with its own code at a7d6c6e, 1 024 series per distribution.
  • Scores: quantile loss over the nine deciles, scaled by the error of the one-step naive forecast on the context, divided by the oracle's. Comparisons across settings use this ratio only.
  • Everything ran in 25 minutes on one node of the MeluXina supercomputer (four A100 GPUs), and the follow-up on shifted leaders in 10 more. The predictions and their outcome, with every criterion and number, are in the repository.
