Note · October 2026

Timestral with a ruler and a pencil

We tested Mistral AI's new Timestral models on series that are simple enough to forecast with a ruler and a pencil, where we know the best forecast that anyone could make. We ran all four sizes, along with five other families of foundation models.

Dorian Joubaud

In short

  • On our 76 main tasks, Timestral 209M comes closest to the best possible forecast. Its error is on average 1.54 times the best possible, against 1.72 for TimesFM 3.0 and 1.86 for Chronos-2.
  • On a random walk or an AR(1), its 80 % band collapses beyond 1 024 steps, because by default the median of each block is fed back as if it had been observed. Much of the band comes back without retraining when quantile paths are fed back instead, as Chronos-2 does.
  • Like Chronos-2 and Toto, it makes little use of a series that runs 96 steps ahead of a random walk, although that series holds the whole future. Timestral's error falls by 96 % once the same series is shifted by its lead and passed as a covariate known in advance.

The best possible forecast

Each of our tasks draws 64 to 256 series from a process that we know, so we also know the best possible forecast, the one that you would make if you knew how the series were generated. Its 80 % band appears as a dashed pair of lines on every plot. We divide the quantile loss of each model by that of this forecast, so a score of 1 is perfect and a score of 2 means twice the error that no model could avoid.

How close each model gets

Besides Timestral in its four sizes, we ran Chronos-2 from Amazon, TimesFM 2.5 and 3.0 from Google, and the five sizes of Toto 2.0 and the previous Toto 1.0 from Datadog.

Timestral 209M does better than Chronos-2 on 53 of the 76 tasks, and its 28M version already matches Chronos-2, a model about four times its size. The gap between Timestral 209M and Chronos-2 comes from the series with 1 % noise, as with 10 % noise the two are level. Timestral was pre-trained on synthetic series alone, before a post-training on real data, so our series may sit closer to its training data than to that of the other models.

Straight lines

With 1 % noise, Timestral forecasts straight lines very well, at 1.2 to 1.5 times the best possible at every size, against 1.7 to 1.9 for Chronos-2. The exception is a line that falls through zero, where Timestral's default clip holds the forecast at zero. TimesFM 3.0 does even better, within 1 % of the best possible on the other lines, because it removes a straight line before forecasting and extends it over the horizon whenever that line explains most of the history. Timestral subtracts a running mean from each patch and divides it by a running spread, which makes all rising lines look alike to it, but TimesFM 2.5 does the same and forecasts them less well.

With only 128 steps of history, Chronos-2's median still follows the line, but its band is twelve times wider than Timestral's and sags far below the line.

Exponential growth

On exponentials, the error of every model grows with the growth rate, and with growth of e⁵ or e¹⁰ over the context, Timestral does worse than Chronos-2. The logarithm of an exponential is a line, so we also let Timestral forecast the logarithm and took the exponential of its quantiles. On these exponentials, its error then stays between 1.1 and 2.1 times the best possible. With 8 192 steps of history or 4 096 steps ahead, the logarithm makes it worse instead.

Breaks, gaps and slow cycles

Timestral follows a recent level shift and crosses a gap in its history at little cost, whereas after a shift Chronos-2's band still reaches back to the old level. On a slow cycle of period 300, TimesFM 2.5 does better with 512 to 2 048 steps of history, and Timestral catches up with 4 096 steps. No model copes with a chirp, whose period keeps shrinking.

Long horizons

Timestral forecasts up to 1 024 steps in one pass. For longer horizons, the package appends the median of each block to the context as if it had been observed. The next block then starts from a known point, and the model loses the uncertainty that has built up so far. On a random walk, the 80 % band contains the truth 79 % of the time over the first block, then 17, 7 and 2 % over the next three.

Chronos-2 also forecasts by blocks of 1 024 steps, but it feeds back nine quantiles, and its band still contains the truth 67 % of the time over the last block. TimesFM 3.0 forecasts the whole horizon in one pass. Over the last block, its band contains the truth 76 % of the time, as often as the best possible band. Its band is about 1.5 times as wide, though. On 12 of the 64 walks, a straight line explains most of the history, so TimesFM 3.0 extends it, while the best forecast of a random walk stays flat.

Why the band collapses

Toto 2.0 shows that the feedback is the cause, because the same weights can forecast a long horizon in one pass or by blocks of 768 steps, feeding the median back between blocks as its README recommends beyond about 1 000 steps. Run by blocks, Toto 2.0 2.5B has a band that contains the truth of a random walk 14 % of the time beyond step 768, against 63 % in one pass. Fed back this way, the median of Toto 2.0 2.5B does stay closer to the truth far out than in one pass, as its README says.

The same happens to Toto 1.0, which predicts no quantiles, so the collapse does not come from predicting them. When each of its 256 paths is fed its own random draws, its band holds and contains the truth 62 % of the time after step 64. When Toto 1.0 is fed the mean of each patch instead, its band contains the truth less than 1 % of the time after step 64.

A fix without retraining

Chronos-2 avoids the collapse by feeding back nine quantile paths instead of one value. After the first block, it appends a different decile of the block to each of nine copies of the context, forecasts the next block from every copy and reads one set of quantiles off the nine forecasts taken together. Its code describes this as a better heuristic "than just using the median to unroll, which leads to uncertainty collapse" (chronos-forecasting, chronos2/pipeline.py). It all happens outside the network, so we reproduced it for Timestral 209M without touching its weights.

Over the last block of the random walk, the band then contains the truth 57 % of the time instead of 2 %. Over the whole 4 096 steps, the error falls from 1.26 to 1.05 times the best possible, while the median barely moves. On the line and the two sines, the error rises by 2 to 45 % at 2 048 or 4 096 steps ahead, and every block after the first takes nine forward passes instead of one. On the random walk, the paths only slow the collapse of the band of the largest Toto 2.0.

A series running ahead of the target

Researchers at Layer 6 found that Chronos-2, Moirai and Toto make little use of a covariate that runs ahead of the target, even when it holds the whole future (Ghoroghchian et al., 2026). We gave our models the target itself 96 steps ahead, observed over the past only, so that its last 96 values are exactly the future to forecast. Timestral, Chronos-2 and both Toto models make little use of it, and TimesFM 3.0 uses only part of it. Shifted back by its lead, the same series becomes a covariate known in advance, which Timestral and Chronos-2 use well on the random walk and the AR(1).

On the random walk and the AR(1), Timestral does use an unshifted series that runs 8 steps ahead, and it takes most of the gain that the best possible forecast gets from it. With 32 steps ahead it takes about a quarter, and with 96 steps none. Toto 2.0 takes no covariates and Toto 1.0 only covariates known in advance, so we gave both the leading series as a second series, as Layer 6 did in SimpleTimeBench.

If you use Timestral

  • Beyond 1 024 steps on series like a random walk or an AR(1), stay within one block or feed back quantile paths, since the default band is far too narrow there.
  • For fast growth, forecast the logarithm and take the exponential of its quantiles, but not with 8 192 steps of history or 4 096 steps ahead, where it made the error worse.
  • Shift a leading series by its lead and pass it as a known covariate, then forecast only as far as the lead, since beyond it Timestral jumps away from the truth.
  • For series that can go below zero, set clip_nonnegative=False, since by default Timestral replaces every negative value of its forecast with zero whenever the past of the series is non-negative. Only the docstring of forecast mentions this default.
  • Keep symmetric_averaging on, as without it the error of Timestral 209M on sines with 1 % noise rises by 9 to 26 %.
Method details
  • About 190 000 series to forecast in 330 tasks, and every model that runs a task sees the same series. The main grid has 512 steps of context and 96 of horizon, with 32 shapes at 1 % and at 10 % noise, relative to the spread of the signal, and 12 random processes. Further grids vary the context from 128 to 8 192 steps and the horizon up to 4 096, and add covariates.
  • The loss of a forecast is its quantile loss over the nine deciles, divided by the mean absolute change between consecutive steps in the history of the series. The score divides this loss by that of the best possible forecast. The quantiles of Chronos-2 and Toto 2.0 can cross, so we sort them, which never increases the loss. The plots show the 10 %, 50 % and 90 % quantiles.
  • Timestral ran with its defaults, then once with symmetric_averaging off and once with clip_nonnegative off, on the logarithm of positive series, and through quantile paths. Chronos-2 and TimesFM 2.5 ran with their recommended settings, the Toto models with their defaults, and TimesFM 3.0 with those of TimesFM3Evaluator, the class that its benchmarks use. We also ran a least-squares line on the whole history, our ruler, and AutoETS and AutoARIMA without seasonality on its last 1 024 steps. On the main grid, AutoETS and AutoARIMA end at 8.7 and 6.7 times the best possible.
  • The defaults of TimesFM 3.0 turn on the same two options as Timestral. With its clip at zero turned off, its error on the main grid falls from 1.72 to 1.65 times the best possible. Its package fills the unknown steps of a shifted leader with the last known value, so we show TimesFM 3.0 with a shifted leader only when the lead covers the whole horizon.
  • Toto 2.0 comes in five sizes, from 4M to 2.5B parameters, and Toto 1.0 has 151M. Both read at most the last 4 096 steps. We read the quantiles of Toto 1.0 off 256 paths drawn with fixed seeds.
  • On SimpleTimeBench, which we regenerated, our Chronos-2 comes close to the value that Layer 6 reported on their measure of bias towards the mean, at 0.70 against 0.69. Our code for the quantile paths gives Chronos-2 the same coverage per block as its own code, to three decimals.
  • We used timestral 1.0.0, chronos-forecasting 2.3.2, timesfm 3.0.2 (for TimesFM 2.5 and 3.0), toto-2 2.0.0, toto-ts 0.1.4 and statsforecast 2.1.1.
